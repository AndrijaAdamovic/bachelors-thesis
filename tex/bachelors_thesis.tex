The goal of this section is to provide introduction to mathematical concepts needed for understanding

\subsection{LTI Dynamical Systems}
Although Willems' Fundamental Lemma stems from the behavioural approach to sytems theory, in this thesis it is formulated using the 
classical state-space LTI system formulation. This formulation is simpler, yet sufficient for the further chapters of the thesis. 

\begin{definition} 
    A discrete-time linear time-invariant (LTI) system is a dynamical system whose dynamics are described by:
    \begin{subequations}
        \begin{align}
            x(t+1) &= Ax(t) + Bu(t) \\
            y(t) &= Cx(t) + Du(t)
        \end{align}
        \label{eq:lti}
    \end{subequations}
    Where \(t \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\) is the time axis, \(x(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{n}\) is the state, \(u(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{m}\) is the input
    and \(y(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{p}\) is the output of the system. \\
    The true state space dimension is $n$ and system matrices \(A \in M_n(\mathbb{R})\), \(B \in M_{n \times m}(\mathbb{R})\), 
    \(C \in M_{p \times n}(\mathbb{R})\), \(D \in M_{p \times m}(\mathbb{R})\).
\end{definition}

In our data-based approach, those matrices (i.e. the system model) are  generally unknown. However, an upper bound on state space dimension is
given $N \geq n$. The concept of a system trajectory is defined now.

\begin{definition}
    A sequence of ordered triples \(\left( u(t), x(t), y(t) \right)_{t=0}^{\infty}\) is called an input-state-output trajectory
    if 

    \[ \begin{bmatrix} x(t+1) \\ y(t) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} A & B \\ C & D  \end{bmatrix} 
    \begin{bmatrix} x(t) \\ u(t)\end{bmatrix}\]

\end{definition}

We can be deduce that, from the linearity of \eqref{eq:lti}, any linear combination of input-state-ouput 
trajectories is itself an input-state-output trajectory i.e. the set of all such trajectories spans a vector space over $\mathbb{R}$.
That space is called the \textit{behaviour} of the system.
 
Additionally, from time invariance \eqref{eq:lti} it can be deduced that:

\[\left( u(t + \tau), x(t + \tau), y(t + \tau) \right)_{t=0}^{\infty}\]

is an input-state-output trajectory for all \(\tau \in \mathbb{N}\)
\begin{definition}
    A sequence of ordered pairs \(\left( u(t), y(t) \right)_{t=0}^{\infty}\) is called an input-output trajectory if 
there exists a sequence of states \(x: \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0} \to \mathbb{R}^{n}\) such that \(\left( u(t), x(t), y(t) \right)_{t=0}^{\infty}\) 
is an input-state-output trajectory.
\end{definition}

In practice we are limited by finite computer memory so it is not possible work with infinite sequences of data like the trajectories defined above, so we
need to take a snippet of the trajectory relevant to us. Motivated by that we state the next definition.

\clearpage
\begin{definition}
    Let \(i,j \in\ \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\ \) such that \(i \leq j\). Given an input-state-output trajectory  \(\left( u(t), x(t), y(t) \right)_{t=0}^{\infty}\),
    the sequence  \(\left( u(t), x(t), y(t) \right)_{t=i}^{j}\) is called a \textit{restricted input-state-output trajectory}. Restricted 
    input-output trajectories are defined in the same way.
\end{definition}

\subsection{The Concepts of Contollability and Observability}

In this section the concepts of controllability and observability will be defined. Since, we defined the LTI system \eqref{eq:lti} 
classically the same will follow for the next definitions. However, although the classical and behavioral notions of controllability 
and observability are closely related, they are not identified unconditionally. Their equivalence depends on how the behavior is represented. 
In particular, when considering input-output behavior, equivalence with the classical state-space definitions typically requires a minimal realization; 
for the full input-state-output behavior, the correspondence is more direct. We will not bother with those comparisons between approaches, more on that 
topic can be found at (TODO: link za clanak/knjigu).

\begin{definition}
    A discrete LTI system \eqref{eq:lti} is called controllable if for any initial state $x(0)$ and any desired state $x_f$, there exists
    a finite-length sequence of inputs 
    \[u(0), u(1), ..., u(N-1)\]
    such that the system reaches \(x(N) = x_f\). Otherwise, the system is said to be uncontrollable.
\end{definition}

In lays terms, it is telling that any state of the system can be reached in finite time by applying finitely many inputs. 

We will now state the famous result for testing system controllability stated and proven by Rudolf. E. Kalman. The pair $(A, B)$ is the pair of matrices from \eqref{eq:lti} and n is
the true state space dimension of the system.

\begin{theorem}(Kalman controllability theorem)
    The pair $(A, B)$ is controllable if and only if the controllability block matrix
    \[ \mathcal{C} = \begin{bmatrix} B & AB & A^{2}B & \cdots & A^{n-1}B\end{bmatrix}\]
    is of full rank.
    \label{thm:ctrb}
\end{theorem}

Now the concept of observability is defined.

\begin{definition}
    A discrete LTI system \eqref{eq:lti} is called observable at $t = 0$ if the initial state x(0) can be uniquely reconstructed from a known 
    restricted input-output trajectory. Otherwise, the system is said to be unobservable.
\end{definition}

Using time-invariance of \eqref{eq:lti} it can be shown that if the system is observable at $t = 0$ then for every  \(t \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\) the state $x(t)$ is reconstructible.
This result is important to control theory when it comes to state estimation (for example Kalman filter) because it gives a necessary condition for exact state estimation.

Now the test for determining observability will be stated.

\begin{theorem}(Kalman observability theorem)
    The pair $(C, A)$ is observable if and only if the observability block matrix
    \[ \mathcal{O} = \begin{bmatrix} C \\ CA \\ CA^{2} \\ \vdots \\ CA^{n-1}\end{bmatrix}\]
    is of full rank.
    \label{thm:obsv}
\end{theorem}

The Kalman criteria for controllability/observability were stated but, there are more different equivalent
statements of which the most used ones are the Popov-Belevitch-Hautus (PBH) tests.

From Theorems~\ref{thm:ctrb} and~\ref{thm:obsv}, a duality between observability and controllability matrices can be seen and
it is not hard to show that using matrix operations we have:
\begin{align*}
    (A, B) \ \mbox{is controllable} &\iff (B^T, A^T) \ \mbox{is observable}.
    \intertext{Similarly,}
    (C, A) \ \mbox{is observable} &\iff (A^T, C^T) \ \mbox{is controllable}.
\end{align*}

\subsection{Persistency of Excitation}
For the Willems' Fundamental Lemma to hold we need a so-called persistently exciting input. By that
it means a rich and diverse enough input such that all modes of the system are expressed in the output.

\subsection*{Hankel Matrices}
For a sequence of vectors from time $t = i$ to $t = j$ we use the following vector notation:

\[s_{[i, j]} = \begin{bmatrix} s(i) \\ s(i+1) \\ \vdots \\ s(j) \end{bmatrix}\]

Where instead of vector $s$ we will use inputs($u$), states($x$) and outputs($y$).

Hankel matrix is a matrix whose entries are constant on the anti-diagonals, contrast to
Toeplitz matrix whose entries are constant on the diagonals. Informally, Hankel matrix is
a mirror image of a Toeplitz matrix.


\begin{definition}
    Given an $T \in \mathbb{N}$, lets consider a restricted input-output trajectory \((u_{[0, T-1]},\allowbreak y_{[0, T-1]})\) of \eqref{eq:lti}. Hankel matrices
    of depth $L \in \{1, ..., T\}$ of these inputs and outputs are given by:

    \[H_{L}(u_{[0, T-1]}) = 
    \begin{bmatrix}
    u(0) & u(1) & \cdots & u(T-L) \\
    u(1) & u(2) & \cdots & u(T-L+1)\\
    \vdots & \vdots &  & \vdots \\
    u(L-1) & u(L) & \cdots & u(T-1)
    \end{bmatrix}\]

    \[H_{L}(y_{[0, T-1]}) = 
    \begin{bmatrix}
    y(0) & y(1) & \cdots & y(T-L) \\
    y(1) & y(2) & \cdots & y(T-L+1)\\
    \vdots & \vdots &  & \vdots \\
    y(L-1) & y(L) & \cdots & y(T-1)
    \end{bmatrix}\]

    \label{def:hankel}
\end{definition}

\clearpage

Generally, we stack input and output Hankel matrices to obtain a single block Hankel matrix used for computation:
\[\begin{bmatrix} H_{L}(u(t)) \\ H_{L}(y(t)) \end{bmatrix} \in M_{(m+p)L \times (T-L+1)}(\mathbb{R}) \]

It is now obvious that every column of the matrices from  \eqref{def:hankel} is a restricted input or output trajectory, depending on 
which matrix is chosen.

Now a precise definition of persistently exciting input is given.

\begin{definition}
    Given an sequence of inputs $u_{[0, T-1]}$ and \(k \in \{1, ..., T\}\). We say that
    the given sequence of inputs is persistently exciting of order k if the Hankel matrix 
    $H_{k}(u_{[0, T-1]})$ is of full row rank.
\end{definition}

We have layed out all the necessary definitions and theorems needed for the statement of the main topic of this 
thesis, the Lemma.
